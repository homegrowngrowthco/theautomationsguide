## Why sequences get flagged (and why it's rarely just the copy)

Most operators assume deliverability problems are a content issue: spam trigger words, too many links, subject lines that sound too salesy. Occasionally that's true. But in my experience, copy is almost always the last thing to fix. The first three failure modes are infrastructure, full stop.

Google and Microsoft's filtering logic now scores the sending infrastructure holistically. Domain age, authentication records, inbox reputation, engagement history, and sending volume patterns all feed the decision before a single word of your email gets evaluated. [Google's email sender guidelines](https://support.google.com/mail/answer/81126) require authenticated domains and below-0.10% spam complaint rates for bulk senders. Miss either threshold and you're filtered at the MX level before any content analysis runs.

The other underrated factor is list quality. Sending to stale, scraped, or unverified lists pushes bounce rates up fast. A 6% hard bounce rate on your sending domain accelerates blacklisting in a way that's genuinely hard to recover from. The irony is that most teams running this problem have been aggressively growing their contact lists while letting the sending infrastructure quietly degrade behind them.

## The audit workflow

The sequence below is the order I actually run these checks. Authentication before list hygiene, list hygiene before sending behavior, infrastructure before copy. Skipping steps or running them in reverse wastes time and masks the real problem.

## The infrastructure layer: where to get sending domains and managed inboxes

[Instantly](/tools/instantly/) handles the campaign orchestration layer well. But it doesn't provision your sending domains or manage the mailbox infrastructure underneath them. That's where [Maildoso](/go/maildoso/) and [Mailforge](/go/mailforge/) come in.

My strong opinion here: don't provision Google Workspace domains at scale for cold outreach. Google knows the pattern, and new domains hitting bulk outreach volumes within days of provisioning is a textbook spam infrastructure signal. Go with a managed provider instead.

[Mailforge](/tools/mailforge/) makes sense if you're managing a large inbox pool and want to keep per-seat costs down. ## What to do when a domain is already flagged

If you're mid-audit and a domain is already on a blocklist or showing a dramatically low Email Health score in Instantly, here's the recovery path.

1. Pause all campaigns from that domain immediately.
2. Drop sending volume to zero from flagged inboxes.
3. Let warm-up run in Instantly for two to three weeks at low volume (20 to 30 per day per inbox).
4. Submit delisting requests to Spamhaus, SORBS, and Barracuda, but don't count on fast resolution.
5. If the domain is under 90 days old and already flagged, retire it. Provisioning a new aged domain through Maildoso is faster than waiting out a recovery cycle on a domain that started life with a strike against it.

The [best CRM for outbound teams](/blog/2026-09-21-best-crm-for-outbound-teams-2026-edition/) post covers how to structure contact management so flagged domains don't bleed into your primary outreach infrastructure. Worth reading if you're also figuring out where Instantly sits in a broader GTM stack.

## The one change that prevents most of this

Here's the pattern I keep seeing. Teams treat sending infrastructure as a one-time setup. Provision the inboxes, connect them to Instantly, set up the sequence, move on. Then warm-up pauses because of a billing issue. DKIM records reset after a domain transfer. List hygiene gets skipped because the campaign is urgent. The whole thing degrades incrementally until you're troubleshooting a fully burned domain six months later.

The fix is simple and boring: a weekly infrastructure check built into your RevOps cadence. Pull the Instantly Email Health dashboard. Verify warm-up is running on all active inboxes. Confirm no new domains have dropped authentication records. Run any new contact batches through ZeroBounce or Bouncer before they enter a sequence. That's fifteen minutes a week. It prevents the multi-hour audit I just walked you through.
