Scheduling is not a sexy RevOps problem. It is one that leaks pipeline in quietly painful ways: unrouted leads, broken CRM sync, reps double-booked because someone grabbed the wrong calendar link. [Cal.com](/go/cal-com/) has become the credible open-source contender to Calendly over the last two years, and the pricing question is legitimate. The list prices look nearly identical, but the total cost story and the capability ceiling are very different depending on your team size, your CRM stack, and whether you want to self-host.

## What each Cal.com plan actually buys you

The [Cal.com pricing page](https://cal.com/pricing) lists three tiers for most teams: Free, Team, and Enterprise. There is also a self-hosted path that changes the math entirely.

**Free** is not a 14-day trial. It is a permanent plan covering one user, unlimited event types, Zoom and Google Meet links, basic email reminders, and public booking pages. For a solo consultant or a founder doing their own outbound, this is a complete product. The limits that actually matter at the individual level: no round-robin, no collective scheduling, no CRM write-back, and no team availability views.

**Team** at $15 per seat per month (annual) unlocks everything a RevOps build actually needs. Round-robin routing, collective events with multiple hosts on a single booking, CRM sync with [HubSpot](/go/hubspot/) and [Salesforce](/go/salesforce/), custom workflows, and the routing forms that let you qualify inbound before showing a calendar. This is the tier you price when you are modeling real GTM usage.

**Enterprise** is contact-sales. The features that push you there are SAML SSO, audit logs, custom domain on managed cloud, dedicated support SLAs, and advanced routing logic with Salesforce-field-based assignment. If you need a rep's Salesforce territory to auto-assign the right calendar, that is an Enterprise conversation.

**Self-hosted community edition** costs zero in license fees. Cal.com is [Apache 2.0 licensed on GitHub](https://github.com/calcom/cal.com). You get the full platform, run it on your own infrastructure, and pay nothing per seat. The honest cost here is engineering time for provisioning, upgrades, and managing the Postgres database. For a scrappy startup with a DevOps-capable founding team, this is a legitimate path. For a 20-person RevOps org that does not want to manage infrastructure, the trade is probably not worth it.

I'd argue the free tier is the most underrated part of Cal.com's model. Most competitors either time-limit it or cripple it enough that you feel the squeeze within a week. Cal.com doesn't do that.

## The true cost: a concrete team-size example

Run the math for a 10-rep SDR team on the managed cloud Team plan.

10 seats x $15 x 12 months = **$1,800 per year**.

Calendly Teams for the same 10 seats at $16 per seat per month = **$1,920 per year**. That is a $120 annual difference. Meaningless at this scale.

What actually shifts the number: if two of those reps need Salesforce territory-based routing (routing an inbound form to the right rep based on the account owner field in SFDC), you are looking at Enterprise pricing on Cal.com. Per the [Cal.com enterprise FAQ](https://cal.com/enterprise), that is a negotiated annual contract typically starting around $3,000 to $5,000 per year for small teams. At that point, [Chili Piper](/go/chili-piper/), which charges per router rather than per seat and bakes Salesforce routing natively into its core product, deserves a real comparison.

The add-on that surprises most teams: **custom email domains for confirmations** and **SMS reminders** are not included in the Team plan. SMS is a per-message add-on. If you are running a high-volume demo booking flow where SMS confirmation materially affects show rates, budget an extra $50 to $200 per month depending on volume.

## Where Cal.com pricing is fair and where it stings

The free individual tier is genuinely one of the most generous in the scheduling category. Cal.com is not using it as a bait-and-switch. It reflects the open-source model: individual users get real value, and the company monetizes at the team and enterprise layer.

The sting is at the enterprise boundary. If you are a mid-market company that needs SAML SSO (which most SOC 2 or enterprise security reviews will require), you are off the Team plan entirely and into a negotiated contract. Calendly has the same pattern, so this is a category issue rather than a Cal.com-specific one. But the jump from $15 per seat to enterprise pricing feels abrupt when SSO is the only feature driving the upgrade.

For [Salesforce](/tools/salesforce/) shops in particular: the Salesforce integration on the Team plan logs meetings and creates contacts, but advanced ownership-based routing (where the form submission looks up the account owner in SFDC and routes accordingly) is Enterprise territory. That feature gap is exactly why [Chili Piper](/tools/chili-piper/) exists and continues to hold market share despite a significantly higher price point.

## The self-host calculus

If your engineering team is already running infrastructure for other tools, self-hosted Cal.com deserves a serious look. The [Cal.com self-hosting documentation](https://cal.com/docs/self-hosting/installation/docker) is thorough and the Docker Compose path is reasonably well maintained. At 20-plus seats, the license savings become material: $3,600 per year at Team pricing versus engineering time spent on upgrades, which per the docs runs to minor version releases on a regular cadence and larger lifts for major releases.

The trap teams fall into: treating self-hosted as "free." The hidden costs are infrastructure (roughly $50 to $150 per month on AWS or GCP for a production-grade setup), engineering time for upgrades, and the opportunity cost when something breaks on a Friday. My read is that the self-host path is right for companies with a dedicated platform engineer and wrong for RevOps teams that need to move fast without babysitting a calendar service.

## The bottom line

Cal.com at $15 per seat is a well-priced scheduling tool for teams up to roughly 20 reps with standard routing needs. The free tier is real. The open-source self-host option is a genuine differentiator that no Calendly equivalent matches. Where [Calendly](/tools/calendly/) holds the edge is in day-one polish and a wider ecosystem of native integrations that work without configuration. Where [Chili Piper](/tools/chili-piper/) is worth the step-up in cost is in Salesforce-native inbound routing at volume, where speed-to-lead is a measurable revenue input rather than a nice-to-have.
