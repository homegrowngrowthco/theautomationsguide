## What you actually get at each tier

Motion's pricing is deceptively simple on the surface. Two plans, one billing frequency toggle, done. But the delta between Individual and Team is mostly about collaboration surface, not AI capability. The AI scheduling engine, which auto-blocks time for tasks and reschedules when your calendar fills up, ships on both plans. That is the main value proposition, and it is not paywalled behind the higher tier.

Where Team earns its price is shared project views, the ability to see teammates' task loads, and collaborative workspaces. For a solo operator or founder, Individual is sufficient. For a RevOps or GTM team where you need visibility into who owns what, Team is the right call.

Per [Motion's own pricing documentation](https://www.usemotion.com/pricing), the feature split is intentionally minimal. Motion is betting you stay for the AI engine, not the feature matrix. I'd argue that is actually a reasonable product bet, but it does mean the tier decision is almost entirely about headcount and collaboration needs, not capability.

## The true cost: running the math

Concrete numbers matter here. Consider a three-person ops team: a RevOps lead, a marketing ops manager, and a CS ops analyst.

**Team plan, annual commitment:**
3 seats x $12/month x 12 months = $432/year

That is $144 per person annually. For comparison, a standard [Calendly](/tools/calendly/) Teams seat runs $16/user/month billed annually, so you are looking at roughly $192/year per person for Calendly on top of that.

**Month-to-month on Individual, same team:**
3 seats x $34/month x 12 months = $1,224/year

That is a $792 premium for the flexibility to cancel. Hard to justify unless your team structure is genuinely uncertain. The annual commitment on Team saves roughly 65% versus month-to-month Individual for the same headcount.

**The cost nobody puts in their spreadsheet:** if your team does not discipline themselves to log tasks into Motion, the AI engine has nothing to schedule. The tool becomes a $144/year calendar skin. The ROI lives entirely in the behavior change, not the feature set. My read is that most teams underestimate how much process change this tool actually requires before it pays off.

There are no usage-based overages, no per-meeting fees, and no AI credit limits per [Motion's pricing page](https://www.usemotion.com/pricing). What you see is what you pay. In a SaaS world full of consumption traps, that is worth crediting them for.

## Where Motion fits in the scheduling stack

Motion does not compete with [Calendly](/tools/calendly/), [Chili Piper](/tools/chili-piper/), or [Cal.com](/tools/cal-com/) directly. Those tools solve inbound booking: a prospect clicks a link, picks a slot, lands on your calendar. Motion solves what happens after that meeting lands, specifically how you organize and auto-prioritize the work that exists around those meetings.

The distinction matters for buying decisions. If the problem is "our AEs cannot get demos booked efficiently," Motion is not your answer. If the problem is "our ops team is constantly context-switching and missing deadlines," Motion has a real case.

That said, there is some scheduling overlap. Motion has a [booking link feature](https://www.usemotion.com/features/booking-links) that lets contacts book meetings directly, similar to Calendly's core use case. For simple inbound scheduling without revenue-critical routing logic, it is adequate. For anything involving round-robin routing, CRM handoff triggers, or [Chili Piper](/tools/chili-piper/)-style Concierge flows, you still need a dedicated scheduling tool. Don't try to make Motion's booking links do that job.

## When to switch (or not)

The signal that it is time to look at Motion is usually consistent: your calendar controls you instead of the other way around. You end an overscheduled week with zero deep work completed, and tasks keep rolling over day after day. Motion's AI engine treats task completion as a first-class calendar event rather than an afterthought. That framing alone is worth something.

The signal that Motion is the wrong move: your team is already disciplined with a PM tool like Asana, Linear, or Notion, and the real problem is pipeline execution rather than personal productivity. In that case, you are buying a solution for a problem you do not have. I'd check your actual task completion rate before signing an annual contract.

Motion's integrations are also thin relative to something like [Clay](/tools/clay/) or [n8n](/tools/n8n/) for automation depth. Motion is a productivity layer. Not an automation engine. Those are genuinely different buying decisions and conflating them is how teams end up with tool overlap and unused seats.

The AI scheduler is only as good as the tasks you feed it. Signing a $432/year Team contract when your team still lives in Slack threads and spreadsheets means you are paying for a calendar sync at a premium price. Validate the behavior change first, then commit.
</MyTake>

## The bottom line on Motion pricing

For a solo RevOps operator or a small ops team that genuinely needs to reclaim deep work time from a fragmented calendar, Motion's $12 to $19 per user per month (annual) is a defensible spend. The AI scheduling engine ships at every tier. No overage surprises. The booking link feature covers basic inbound scheduling without forcing a separate tool purchase. Where it falls short: CRM-connected routing, deep automation, and teams who are not yet disciplined about logging tasks. If your real problem is inbound meeting booking and revenue routing, [Chili Piper](/tools/chili-piper/) or Calendly are still the right answer there. Motion does not change that.
